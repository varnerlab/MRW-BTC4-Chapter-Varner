\section{Integrative Flux Bounds}\label{sec:gateways}

Reaction bounds can include several types of condition-specific data. For
reaction $j$, the bound is written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:general-bound}
  -\delta_j\Bigl[V^{\circ}_{\max,j}\,(e/e^{\circ})\,\theta_j(\cdot)\,f_j(\cdot)\Bigr]
  \;\le\; \hat v_j \;\le\;
  V^{\circ}_{\max,j}\,(e/e^{\circ})\,\theta_j(\cdot)\,f_j(\cdot),
\end{equation}
where $V^{\circ}_{\max,j}$ is a reference capacity with units of flux. The
other factors are dimensionless. The reversibility factor $\delta_j$ sets
the reverse direction. The reference capacity $V^{\circ}_{\max,j}$ and
saturation factor $f_j$ describe enzyme capacity and substrate saturation.
The enzyme-abundance ratio $(e/e^{\circ})$ compares the current enzyme
concentration $e$ with the reference concentration $e^{\circ}$, and the
allosteric factor $\theta_j$ describes enzyme activity.
Transcriptional and translational controls $u_j$ and $w_j$ act through the
expression model and change the enzyme-abundance ratio $(e/e^{\circ})$. They
are distinct from the allosteric factor $\theta_j$. When data are unavailable,
the corresponding factor is set to one. Measurements or mechanistic models
can then replace that default.

The reversibility factor $\delta_j$ has two values:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thermo-gate}
  \delta_j \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    1, & \text{reaction $j$ reversible},\\[2pt]
    0, & \text{reaction $j$ irreversible},
  \end{cases}.
\end{equation}
When the reversibility factor $\delta_j=1$, the lower bound is negative and
reverse flux is allowed. When the reversibility factor $\delta_j=0$, the lower
bound is zero. A simple assignment compares the standard transformed reaction
Gibbs energy $\Delta_{\mathrm r}G_j^{\prime\circ}$ with a heuristic
reversibility threshold $\Delta_{\mathrm r}G_j^{\prime *}$. A full
thermodynamic analysis instead propagates stated activity ranges and
uncertainties through the reaction Gibbs energy, which is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reaction-gibbs}
  \Delta_{\mathrm r}G_j^{\prime}
  = \Delta_{\mathrm r}G_j^{\prime\circ}
  + RT_{\mathrm{abs}}\ln Q_j,
  \qquad
  Q_j = \prod_i a_i^{\sigma_{ij}},
\end{equation}
where the gas constant $R=8.314\times10^{-3}$ kJ\,mol$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$,
$T_{\mathrm{abs}}$ is absolute temperature in K, $Q_j$ is the dimensionless
reaction quotient, $a_i$ is the dimensionless activity of species $i$
relative to its standard state, and $\sigma_{ij}$ is the stoichiometric
coefficient of species $i$ in reaction $j$, with negative values for reactants
and positive values for products. The prime denotes a
biochemical transformed quantity evaluated under specified reference
conditions, including pH and ionic strength. Reaction direction is tested by
whether the reaction Gibbs energy $\Delta_{\mathrm r}G_j^{\prime}$ can change
sign over the allowed activity ranges \cite{Henry2007}. The component
contribution method provides consistent standard transformed Gibbs-energy
estimates and uncertainties \cite{Noor2013}, and eQuilibrator makes these
estimates available for metabolic calculations \cite{Beber2022}.

Enzyme kinetics set the forward capacity. The reference capacity is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vmax}
  V^{\circ}_{\max,j} \;=\; k^{\circ}_{\mathrm{cat},j}\,e^{\circ},
\end{equation}
where $k^{\circ}_{\mathrm{cat},j}$ is the turnover number and $e^{\circ}$
is a reference enzyme concentration. BRENDA reports turnover numbers
\cite{Chang2021}, and BioNumbers reports cellular abundance scales
\cite{Milo2010}. The saturation factor gives the fraction of
the reference capacity $V^{\circ}_{\max,j}$ available at the current substrate
concentration.
For a single-substrate enzyme, the reaction mechanism is written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mm-mechanism}
  E + S_j \;\underset{k_{-1}}{\overset{k_1}{\rightleftharpoons}}\; [ES]
  \;\overset{k_{\mathrm{cat}}}{\longrightarrow}\; E + P,
\end{equation}
where $E$ is free enzyme, $S_j$ is substrate, $[ES]$ is the enzyme--substrate
complex, and $P$ is product. The rate constants $k_1$, $k_{-1}$, and
$k_{\mathrm{cat}}$ describe substrate association, substrate dissociation,
and catalysis, respectively. The quasi-steady-state
approximation and enzyme conservation, $[E]_T=[E]+[ES]$, give
$[ES]=[E]_T[S_j]/(K_{M,j}+[S_j])$, where $[E]_T$ is total enzyme
concentration and $K_{M,j}=(k_{-1}+k_{\mathrm{cat}})/k_1$ is the Michaelis
constant. With the reaction rate $v_j=k_{\mathrm{cat}}[ES]$ and maximum rate
$V_{\max,j}=k_{\mathrm{cat}}[E]_T$, the Michaelis--Menten law is given by
\cite{MichaelisMenten1913}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mm}
  v_j \;=\; V_{\max,j}\,\frac{[S_j]}{K_{M,j}+[S_j]}
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  f_j([S_j]) \;=\; \frac{v_j}{V_{\max,j}}
  \;=\; \frac{[S_j]}{K_{M,j}+[S_j]}\;\in[0,1],
\end{equation}
where $[S_j]$ is the substrate concentration. The saturation factor $f_j$
approaches zero at low substrate and one at saturation. Because the saturation
factor $f_j$ depends on the substrate concentration $[S_j]$, the bound becomes
state-dependent.

Gene expression sets enzyme abundance. The balances for transcript $m_j$ and
protein $p_j$ are given by:
\begin{align}
  \dot m_j &= r_{X,j}\,u_j - (\theta_{m,j}+\mu)\,m_j + \lambda_j,
  \label{eq:mrna}\\
  \dot p_j &= r_{L,j}\,w_j\,m_j - (\theta_{p,j}+\mu)\,p_j,
  \label{eq:protein}
\end{align}
where $r_{X,j}$ and $r_{L,j}$ are transcription and translation
capacities, $u_j$ is the dimensionless transcriptional control, $w_j$ is the
dimensionless translational control,
$\theta_{m,j}$ and $\theta_{p,j}$ are degradation constants, and
$\lambda_j$ is the basal transcription rate. The growth rate $\mu$ adds to
both loss rates. At steady state, the solutions are:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expression-ss}
  m^{\star}_j = \frac{r_{X,j}u_j+\lambda_j}{\theta_{m,j}+\mu},
  \qquad
  p^{\star}_j = \frac{r_{L,j}w_j\,m^{\star}_j}{\theta_{p,j}+\mu},
  \qquad
  (e/e^{\circ}) = \frac{p^{\star}_j}{e^{\circ}}.
\end{equation}
Gene--protein--reaction rules map the steady-state protein abundance
$p_j^\star$ to catalytic activity. These
rules represent single enzymes, multisubunit complexes, and isozymes.
Measured transcript or protein abundance can also set the enzyme-abundance
ratio $(e/e^{\circ})$ directly and produce condition-specific capacities.

Active and inactive configurations provide one statistical-mechanical
description of allosteric, transcriptional, and translational control
(Fig.~\ref{fig:active-subset}). For an enzyme, promoter, or translation
complex, the
control is the statistical weight of the active set divided by the weight of
all configurations. Let $\mathcal S_q$ be the configurations for process $q$,
where $q$ denotes
catalysis, transcription, or translation. For a configuration
$s\in\mathcal S_q$ with energy $\epsilon_s$, the state weight, partition
function, and state probability are given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:partition}
  \omega_s(\mathbf x)
    = g_s(\mathbf x)e^{-\beta\epsilon_s},
  \qquad
  Z_q(\mathbf x)
    = \sum_{r\in\mathcal S_q}\omega_r(\mathbf x),
  \qquad
  p_s(\mathbf x)
    = \frac{\omega_s(\mathbf x)}{Z_q(\mathbf x)},
\end{equation}
where $\beta=1/(k_BT_{\mathrm{abs}})$ is the inverse thermal energy,
$k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant, $T_{\mathrm{abs}}$ is absolute temperature,
and $Z_q$ is the partition function. The concentration factor
$g_s(\mathbf x)$ includes ligand concentration, binding stoichiometry, and
degeneracy. For example, a state with binding stoichiometry $n$, effector
concentration $x$, and concentration scale $K$ has a concentration factor
proportional to $(x/K)^n$.

Let $\mathcal A_q\subseteq\mathcal S_q$ contain the active configurations
and let $\mathcal I_q=\mathcal S_q\setminus\mathcal A_q$ contain the
inactive configurations. The control $\Phi_q$ is the probability of the active
set:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:active-state-control}
  \Phi_q(\mathbf x)
    = \sum_{s\in\mathcal A_q}p_s(\mathbf x)
    = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal A_q}
        g_s(\mathbf x)e^{-\beta\epsilon_s}}
      {\displaystyle\sum_{r\in\mathcal S_q}
        g_r(\mathbf x)e^{-\beta\epsilon_r}}
    = \frac{Z_{\mathcal A_q}}
      {Z_{\mathcal A_q}+Z_{\mathcal I_q}}.
\end{equation}
Here $Z_{\mathcal C}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal C}\omega_s$ is the partition sum
over a configuration subset $\mathcal C$. Active states appear in the
numerator and denominator; inactive states appear only in the denominator. Therefore
$0\leq\Phi_q\leq1$ for the control $\Phi_q$. An effector increases or
decreases the control $\Phi_q$ according
to the states it stabilizes. Choose configuration $0$ as a reference, define
the energy difference $\Delta\epsilon_s=\epsilon_s-\epsilon_0$, and denote the
effective relative weight of state $s$ by $K_{\star,s}$. The relative weights
are then given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:effective-state-weight}
  K_{\star,s}
    \equiv \frac{\omega_s/g_s}{\omega_0/g_0}
    = e^{-\beta\Delta\epsilon_s},
  \qquad
  \frac{\omega_s}{\omega_0}
    = \frac{g_s(\mathbf x)}{g_0(\mathbf x)}K_{\star,s}.
\end{equation}
With the reference weight $\omega_0$ and reference concentration factor $g_0$
set to one, the state weight is
$\omega_s=g_s(\mathbf x)K_{\star,s}$. A term such as $K_2f_{TL}$ combines
the energetic factor $K_2=e^{-\beta\Delta\epsilon_2}$ with the available
ligand-bound transcription-factor fraction $f_{TL}$. Fitted state-weight
parameters $K_1$ and $K_2$ may also include constant polymerase concentration,
standard-state terms, or other fixed contributions. They need not be measured
dissociation constants.

Moon, Voigt, and coworkers used this construction for inducible promoters
\cite{Moon2012}. The Lux promoter has state weights $1$, $K_1$, and
$K_2f_{TL}$ for the empty, polymerase-bound, and polymerase--LuxR-bound
states. Both polymerase-bound states permit transcription. The Tac promoter
has state weights $1$, $K_1$, and $K_2f_T$ for the empty, polymerase-bound, and
repressor-bound states. Only the polymerase-bound state permits
transcription. Normalization gives the promoter controls:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moon-promoter-control}
  u_{\mathrm{Lux}}
    = \frac{K_1+K_2f_{TL}}{1+K_1+K_2f_{TL}},
  \qquad
  u_{\mathrm{Tac}}
    = \frac{K_1}{1+K_1+K_2f_T}.
\end{equation}
Here the fraction $f_{TL}$ is ligand-bound transcription factor and the
fraction $f_T$ is ligand-free transcription factor. Increasing inducer adds
weight to an active Lux state and removes weight from an inactive Tac state. Both controls are
special cases of Equation~\eqref{eq:active-state-control}. A two-state model
gives a familiar special case. If the inactive state has
weight $\omega_I=1$ and the effector-stabilized active state has weight
$\omega_A=(x/K)^n e^{-\beta\Delta\epsilon}$, then the control is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-state-control}
  \Phi_q(x)
    = \frac{(x/K)^n e^{-\beta\Delta\epsilon}}
      {1+(x/K)^n e^{-\beta\Delta\epsilon}},
\end{equation}
where the energy difference $\Delta\epsilon$ is the active-state energy
relative to the inactive state. If the effector instead stabilizes the inactive state, its weight
appears only in the denominator and the control decreases with the effector
concentration $x$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:active-state-control} gives each process-specific factor:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:control-mapping}
  \theta_j = \Phi_{\mathrm{cat},j},
  \qquad
  \bar u_j = \Phi_{\mathrm{TX},j},
  \qquad
  w_j = \Phi_{\mathrm{TL},j}.
\end{equation}
The allosteric factor $\theta_j$ multiplies catalytic capacity in
Equation~\eqref{eq:general-bound}. The total transcriptional control
$\bar u_j$ controls transcription, and the translational control $w_j$
controls translation in
Equation~\eqref{eq:protein}. These controls are distinct from the
degradation constants $\theta_{m,j}$ and $\theta_{p,j}$. Split the
transcriptionally active set into regulated and basal states.
Let $\mathcal A_{\mathrm{TX}}^{\mathrm{reg}}$ contain the regulated states
and $\mathcal A_{\mathrm{TX}}^{\mathrm{basal}}$ contain states that support
transcription without the regulated input. The resulting split is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:control-split}
  \bar u_j
    = \frac{Z_{\mathcal A_{\mathrm{TX}}^{\mathrm{reg}}}}{Z_{\mathrm{TX}}}
    + \frac{Z_{\mathcal A_{\mathrm{TX}}^{\mathrm{basal}}}}{Z_{\mathrm{TX}}}
    = u_j + u_j^{\dagger},
  \qquad
  \lambda_j \equiv r_{X,j}u_j^{\dagger}.
\end{equation}
Here $Z_{\mathrm{TX}}$ is the total transcriptional partition function. The
regulated transcriptional control $u_j$ enters Equation~\eqref{eq:mrna}.
The basal control $u_j^{\dagger}$ defines the basal transcription rate
$\lambda_j$. Moon, Voigt, and coworkers used this
promoter-state construction \cite{Moon2012}. Adhikari and coworkers
extended it to transcriptional and translational control
\cite{Adhikari2020}.

These factors require thermodynamic, kinetic, expression, and regulatory
data. When data are unavailable, set the saturation factor $f_j\approx1$, the
enzyme-abundance ratio $(e/e^{\circ})\approx1$, and the allosteric factor
$\theta_j\approx1$. This gives the baseline bound:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:simplified-bound}
  -\delta_j\,V^{\circ}_{\max,j} \;\le\; \hat v_j \;\le\; V^{\circ}_{\max,j}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:simplified-bound} retains only reversibility and
reference capacity. Equation~\eqref{eq:general-bound} shows which omitted
factors require additional data. Each factor has a precedent in
constraint-based modeling. Thermodynamics-
based flux analysis uses free energies and metabolite activity ranges to
exclude infeasible states \cite{Henry2007}. Enzyme-constrained FBA uses
turnover numbers and enzyme abundance; GECKO applies this approach at genome
scale \cite{Sanchez2017}. Metabolism-and-expression models couple
transcription and translation to metabolism \cite{OBrien2013}. Regulatory
FBA uses Boolean expression rules \cite{Covert2001}, whereas PROM uses
data-derived interaction probabilities \cite{Chandrasekaran2010}. The
allosteric factor $\theta_j$ represents direct regulation of enzyme
activity. Equation~\eqref{eq:general-bound} places these established terms
in one notation. The balances above use aggregate transcription and
translation capacities $r_{X,j}$ and $r_{L,j}$. A sequence-resolved model can
instead calculate
nucleotide, amino-acid, and energy demands from each gene and protein
sequence and add those reactions to the stoichiometric matrix $\mathbf S$
\cite{AllenPalsson2003}.
Expression then becomes part of the flux calculation. Vilkhovoy and
coworkers used this construction for cell-free protein synthesis
\cite{Vilkhovoy2018}.
