\section{Integration in Practice}\label{sec:capstones}

The urea-cycle and feedback examples opened the gateways of
Equation~\eqref{eq:general-bound} one at a time on networks with a
single degree of freedom, $n-m=1$. Two reported systems show those
gateways operating together at genome scale, where the large right
null space makes informative bounds essential
(Section~\ref{sec:lp}). The first uses measurements to couple
metabolism, expression, and regulation in a dynamic cell-free model;
the second modifies bounds to design a glycan-producing strain.

Vilkhovoy and coworkers constructed a sequence-specific model of
\emph{E.~coli} cell-free protein synthesis in which transcription and
translation reactions derived from the target DNA and mRNA sequences
enter the stoichiometric matrix alongside metabolism
\cite{Vilkhovoy2018}. The effective biophysical model of Adhikari and
coworkers supplies the active fractions of promoter and ribosome
capacity \cite{Adhikari2020}, extending the control function
$\theta_j$ of Equations~\eqref{eq:partition}
and~\eqref{eq:control-split} from one feedback loop to every regulated
step. The resulting dynamic model recomputes thermodynamic, kinetic,
expression, and regulatory bounds as the reaction proceeds
\cite{Vilkhovoy2023} and is evaluated against trajectories of 63
metabolites together with mRNA, protein, and enzyme-activity data.
These measurements also expose what ordinary flux balance analysis
discards. Although cell-free reactions have $\mu=0$, their molecular
pools vary throughout the batch, so the pseudo-steady-state reduction
from Equation~\eqref{eq:mole-balance} to
Equation~\eqref{eq:honest-constraint} does not apply. With no membrane
separating the reaction volume from an inferred intracellular
compartment, accumulation is observed directly rather than
reconstructed from uptake or secretion rates. Cell-free integration
therefore provides a setting in which the accumulation term omitted
by Equation~\eqref{eq:sv0} can be measured instead of assumed away.

Wayman and coworkers used the framework in the opposite direction.
They grafted the N-linked glycosylation pathway of
\emph{Campylobacter jejuni} onto a genome-scale reconstruction of
\emph{E.~coli} metabolism and searched for gene deletions that couple
designer-glycan production to growth \cite{Wayman2019}. Each candidate
knockout collapses a reaction bound to zero and defines a new box
$[\boldsymbol\ell,\mathbf u]$ in which the linear program is re-solved
before a strain is built. More generally, attenuating or
over-expressing an enzyme can lower or raise the $(e/e^{\circ})$ factor
of Equation~\eqref{eq:general-bound} without eliminating the reaction.
The best single knockout identified by the model increased glycan
production roughly threefold when built and tested, although the
model selected the deletion rather than predicting the observed yield
increase. Here the bounds serve not as a report of cellular behavior
but as levers for changing it.

The two capstones thus apply Equation~\eqref{eq:general-bound} in
opposite directions. Measurements narrow the cell-free gateways to
estimate flux, whereas engineering interventions narrow the glycan
gateways to design it; once the bounds are fixed, both re-solve the
same linear program. Design additionally requires choosing which
bounds to move, a discrete search over deletions or expression changes
that can be formulated through bilevel programming
\cite{Burgard2003}. Estimation and design share the evaluation of a
bounded flux model, but only design must first search for the
intervention.
